Why Machiavelli Is the Most Misunderstood Thinker in History: Ada Palmer

We use 'Machiavellian' as a synonym for ruthless, psychopathic scheming. But University of Chicago historian Ada Palmer reveals that Niccolò Machiavelli was actually a passionate republican diplomat who spent his life trying to save Florence from mercenary warlords and hereditary tyrants.

Few historical figures have suffered as total a semantic inversion as Niccolò Machiavelli. To modern ears, his name evokes backstabbing corporate politics and authoritarian cruelty. Yet the historical Machiavelli was tortured on the rack for defending the Florentine Republic, refused to enrich himself in public office, and died in poverty.

In this masterclass on Renaissance politics, historian and novelist Ada Palmer walked Dwarkesh Patel through the brutal geopolitical landscape that produced *The Prince* and *The Discourses*.

Insight 1 Florence was a tiny democratic mouse between monstrous imperial cats

Renaissance Italy was not an idyllic cradle of art; it was an active war zone where the Kingdom of France, the Holy Roman Empire, and the Papacy were dismembering independent city-states. Florence had no standing army and relied on mercenary captains (*condottieri*) who routinely switched sides mid-battle for a higher bribe.

"Machiavelli lived through the trauma of watching foreign armies march across Italy with zero resistance. He wasn't advising rulers to be cruel for fun; he was asking: what does it take for a tiny, fragile self-governing republic to avoid being sacked, raped, and conquered by massive despotic monarchies?"

— Ada Palmer, Jun 2026
Insight 2 Why popes were warlords and common people demanded nepotism

Palmer illuminated why medieval institutions looked so corrupt to modern eyes. A pope had a fragile tenure: once he died, his faction was purged. The only people a Renaissance pope could trust to command the fortress of Castel Sant'Angelo or lead papal cavalry were his literal blood relatives.

Nepotism wasn't viewed as a moral flaw — it was the only credible commitment mechanism available in a world before bureaucratic legal contracts existed.

Insight 3 Cesare Borgia brought terror to the nobility, but peace to the peasants

Machiavelli's notorious praise of Cesare Borgia is usually treated as evidence of depravity. Palmer contextualized Borgia's campaign in the Romagna: the local barons were predatory bandits who murdered and extorted the peasant population with impunity. When Borgia arrived, he slaughtered the petty warlords and installed a strict, uncorrupt magistrate (Ramiro de Lorqua).

For the average farmer, Borgia's ruthless central authority brought the first decades of physical security and rule of law they had ever experienced.

Insight 4 The Prince was a triage manual, but The Discourses was his true philosophy

Most readers only ever open *The Prince*, written while Machiavelli was exiled and desperate. His magnum opus, *Discourses on Livy*, is an uncompromising defense of civic republicanism, separation of powers, citizen militias, and popular sovereignty. He argued that the wisdom of the common people was consistently superior to the capricious whims of princes.

The bottom line

Machiavelli was not an advocate for tyranny — he was a political scientist performing an autopsy on why republics die. His greatest lesson remains as vital today as in 1513: ideals without institutional power are instantly devoured by power without ideals.
