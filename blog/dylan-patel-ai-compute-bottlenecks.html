The 3 Bottlenecks That Could Kill AI Scaling — Dylan Patel Explains

SemiAnalysis founder Dylan Patel spent 2.5 hours walking through exactly why ASML's EUV machines, HBM memory production, and power infrastructure will each independently constrain AI compute scaling through 2030 — and what that means for the labs racing to build AGI.

Source Episode

Full transcript →

The four big hyperscalers — Amazon, Microsoft, Google, and Meta — have announced a combined $600 billion in AI capital expenditures for the coming years. OpenAI just raised $110 billion. Anthropic raised $30 billion. If you take these numbers seriously, it implies a need for compute capacity that is, by Dylan Patel's calculation, simply not available yet.

Dylan Patel is the founder of SemiAnalysis, one of the most closely-read research shops in the semiconductor industry. He appeared on Dwarkesh Patel's podcast for a 2.5-hour technical deep dive that covered, in extraordinary detail, exactly where the AI compute supply chain is about to break.

Bottleneck 1 ASML — The single point of failure for advanced logic chips

Every advanced AI chip — NVIDIA's Blackwell, AMD's MI-series, Google's TPUs — requires extreme ultraviolet (EUV) lithography to manufacture. EUV machines are made by exactly one company in the world: ASML, headquartered in the Netherlands. ASML's machines cost $150-200 million each, take years to build, and involve a supply chain of tens of thousands of components.

Patel's argument: by 2030, ASML will be the single binding constraint on AI compute scaling. The company simply cannot manufacture EUV machines fast enough to satisfy the implied demand from current CapEx commitments. This isn't a question of willingness — it's a question of physical manufacturing limits on an extraordinarily complex product.

"ASML will be the #1 constraint for AI compute scaling by 2030. You can't just spin up another ASML. It took decades to build that supply chain."

— Dylan Patel

Critically, this bottleneck applies even if you try to work around it. TSMC's older 7nm fabs don't require EUV — but 7nm chips are far less efficient for AI workloads than leading-edge 3nm and 2nm chips, meaning you'd need far more of them to achieve the same compute. And there's simply not enough older fab capacity either.

Bottleneck 2 HBM Memory — The enormous incoming memory crunch

High Bandwidth Memory (HBM) is the type of memory that sits on AI chips and allows them to move data fast enough for large model inference. Without sufficient HBM, you can have all the compute in the world and still be bottlenecked on getting data to it fast enough.

The memory market is currently dominated by three companies: SK Hynix, Samsung, and Micron. And HBM is one of the most complex memory products to manufacture — it requires stacking multiple dies vertically with microscopic connections between them. Yield rates are lower than for standard DRAM, capacity expansion is slow, and demand is growing faster than anyone projected two years ago.

Patel walked through his analysis showing that HBM supply is already constrained and that the constraint gets significantly worse as inference demand scales. Anthropic's recent scramble to acquire compute capacity at premium prices is partly explained by this: the best compute is locked up in long-term contracts, and anyone coming to market late pays a significant premium — or accepts lower-quality hardware.

"The enormous incoming memory crunch is real. Inference at scale is memory-bandwidth constrained, not compute constrained. And HBM capacity can't be conjured quickly."

— Dylan Patel
Bottleneck 3 Power — 50 gigawatts of implied demand, 20 coming online

At current rental prices for AI compute, the hyperscalers' announced CapEx implies a need for roughly 50 gigawatts of power — but only about 20 gigawatts of new capacity is actually being added in the US in 2026. The gap is partly temporal (some CapEx is paying for capacity in future years) and partly structural (power grid permitting is genuinely slow).

Patel was more optimistic here than on the semiconductor constraints. He thinks power will not be the binding constraint, because data center operators are being creative: long-term power purchase agreements, dedicated gas turbines, nuclear power letters of intent, and in some cases designs that run at lower utilization to stay within grid capacity.

But he flagged an important nuance: while total power is manageable, cooling is becoming a harder problem. The density of AI chips per rack is increasing faster than cooling infrastructure can scale, and the most efficient approaches (liquid cooling, immersion cooling) require significant lead time to build and a different construction expertise than traditional air-cooled data centers.

The counterintuitive finding: an H100 is worth more today than in 2022

One of the most interesting claims in the entire conversation: despite being a four-year-old chip in a market where Blackwell is the current generation, an H100 GPU has actually appreciated in value since its launch. This seems impossible on its face — technology depreciates.

Patel's explanation turns on supply scarcity. The total addressable market for what you can do with an H100 has grown enormously, because the models it can serve (GPT-4 class and above) are now capable of tasks that generate real economic value. Meanwhile, the supply of H100s is fixed — you can't manufacture more of a product that's two generations old. And because new-generation Blackwell chips are immediately sold out, anyone who needs inference capacity is paying premium rates even for older hardware.

"H100 is worth more today than it was three years ago. The value of a GPU is now predicated on the value that the best models can get out of it — not on the comparative price of newer hardware."

— Dylan Patel

This has an important implication for the financial models around AI infrastructure: the bear case that GPU depreciation would devastate cloud providers' balance sheets may be fundamentally wrong. As long as demand for inference outpaces supply of compute, even older hardware maintains value.

The bottom line

Dylan Patel's analysis suggests that the $600 billion in AI CapEx is real and the demand for compute is real — but the physical supply chain is genuinely constrained in ways that can't be solved by writing a bigger check. ASML machines, HBM production, and power infrastructure all face multi-year expansion timelines. The labs that secured compute contracts early (OpenAI, with its aggressive Microsoft and Oracle deals) have a significant structural advantage over those that were more conservative (Anthropic).
