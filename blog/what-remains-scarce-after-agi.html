What Remains Scarce After AGI? Alex Imas & Phil Trammell on Post-Labor Economics

When intelligence and physical labor become essentially free, standard economic models break down. Economists Alex Imas and Phil Trammell break down what will actually hold value in an era of machine abundance: land, energy, raw matter, and positional status.

Full transcript →

Classical economics is built on the fundamental assumption that human labor is the scarce, irreducible input to production. What happens when digital agents can write every line of software, design every circuit, and steer robots to manufacture every physical good?

University of Chicago professor Alex Imas and Oxford economist Phil Trammell joined Dwarkesh Patel to explore the economic architecture of a post-labor civilization.

Insight 1 The labor share of GDP plunges toward zero while capital share approaches 100%

For two centuries, the division of national income between labor and capital remained famously steady (roughly 70% labor, 30% capital — Bowley's Law). Imas and Trammell argue that AGI will completely shatter this equilibrium:

"Once human cognition is no longer a bottleneck to production, wages as a share of output collapse toward zero. If you don't own capital — compute shares, land titles, mineral extraction rights — your economic bargaining power in the market evaporates."

— Phil Trammell, Jun 2026
Insight 2 The 'Messy Middle' transition is where political breakdown occurs

The danger is not the final post-scarcity steady state; it is the decade-long transition where 40% of white-collar and knowledge workers are automated before physical robotics reaches full deployment. In this phase, wealth concentration explodes faster than fiscal policy can adapt, putting unprecedented stress on democratic governance.

Insight 3 Positional goods and human authenticity become the supreme luxuries

When physical commodities, media, and digital services can be infinitely replicated at marginal cost, where does consumer spending migrate? Positional goods that are strictly finite by definition: beachfront property, historic artifacts, exclusive club memberships, and human-crafted experiences whose value comes precisely from being inefficiently made by real people.

Insight 4 Why aggregate demand won't collapse: Machines consume too

Luddite critiques often ask: 'If workers have no jobs, who will buy the products?' Trammell explained that autonomous firms themselves become the primary consumers: compute clusters demand cooling water, power generation, silicon substrates, and orbital satellite launches. The economy doesn't stall; it expands along an automated business-to-business frontier.

The bottom line

Abundance does not mean the end of economics; it means the end of human labor as the unit of account. Preparing for AGI requires rethinking property rights, sovereign wealth funds, and land taxation before the capital share absorbs the entire global economy.
