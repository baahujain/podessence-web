Why Leonardo Was a Saboteur, Gutenberg Went Broke, and Florence Won: Ada Palmer

The Renaissance wasn't an effortless flowering of human genius; it was a chaotic collision of bankrupt inventors, paranoid city-states, and subversive artists. Ada Palmer unpacks the wild economics of the printing press, Leonardo's defense contracts, and Florentine sortition.

Popular history paints the Italian Renaissance in pastel tones: Michelangelo painting ceilings, Leonardo sketching flying machines, and Gutenberg launching the information age. But the reality on the ground was a dog-eat-dog struggle of high-stakes patent litigation, espionage, and brutal political experimentation.

In this dazzling discussion, historian Ada Palmer dismantled the mythology around the era's greatest icons.

Insight 1 Leonardo da Vinci: The pacifist defense contractor who sabotaged his own blueprints

Leonardo made his living not as a painter, but as a military engineer pitching siege engines and armored tanks to despots like Ludovico Sforza and Cesare Borgia. Yet Palmer revealed that when you analyze Leonardo's military notebooks, his mechanical drawings contain deliberate engineering errors:

"Leonardo would draw a giant armored tank or scythed chariot with gears that would lock if turned in the forward direction. He was taking huge cash retainers from warmongering dukes, but he was a committed vegetarian pacifist who actively feared the destruction his inventions could unleash. He designed weapons that were literally sabotaged on the parchment."

— Ada Palmer, Mar 2026
Insight 2 Why Gutenberg went broke despite inventing the most valuable tool in history

Johannes Gutenberg developed movable type, punch-cutting, and oil-based ink — unleashing the greatest communication revolution since written language. Yet he was sued by his financier Johann Fust, had his print shop foreclosed upon, and died in financial ruin.

Palmer pointed out the structural problem: Gutenberg invested years of Capex in casting lead typesets before printing a single Bible. Once printed, market liquidity was non-existent — only a handful of cathedrals and wealthy merchants could afford a Latin folio. He had solved the technology of production, but distribution, credit lines, and customer purchasing power were still stuck in the Middle Ages.

Insight 3 Florence chose its government by lottery (sortition) to defeat polarization

The Florentine Republic did not elect its leaders via political campaigns. They believed elections inevitably favored wealthy oligarchs with PR machines. Instead, they put the names of thousands of qualified citizens into leather bags (*borse*) and pulled names out at random to serve two-month terms in the Signoria.

This radical sortition system prevented any single family from entrenching permanent administrative control — until the Medici family figured out how to subvert the lottery bagger.

Insight 4 The Roman Inquisition accidentally invented modern academic peer review

To censor dangerous heretical ideas, the Catholic Church established the Index of Prohibited Books. But to ban a book, they had to hire multiple independent theological scholars to read the manuscript, cross-reference sources, write formal critiques, and propose revisions. This institutionalized bureaucratic protocol of multi-reviewer assessment inadvertently became the exact template for modern scientific peer review.

The bottom line

Technology and genius do not operate in a vacuum. Gutenberg went broke because finance lagged engineering; Leonardo sabotaged his weapons because ethics clashed with employment. Progress is always messy, imperfect, and intensely human.
