Why Russia Lost the Cold War: 5 Arguments You Haven’t Heard

Military historian Sarah Paine gave Dwarkesh Patel a masterclass on why the Soviet Union collapsed. The popular answer — Reagan did it — is at best a partial truth. The real story involves Nixon's gambit with China, an accidental bureaucratic mistake that opened the Berlin Wall, and an economy built entirely on lies.

Source Episode
Sarah Paine — Why Russia Lost the Cold War  ·  Dwarkesh Podcast  ·  Dec 19, 2025  ·  1h 54m
Full transcript →

Sarah Paine is a military historian at the Naval War College who has spent decades studying the Soviet collapse, the Cold War's geopolitical architecture, and the broader patterns of great-power competition. In late 2025, she sat down with Dwarkesh Patel for nearly two hours to walk through every serious explanation for why the Soviet Union lost — and to stress-test each one against the historical record.

Her framing from the start: there are many convincing partial explanations, and partisans of each tend to overstate their case. The real picture is a cascade of compounding failures. Here are the five most important arguments she covers, with her assessment of each.

Argument 1 Reagan did it — the arms race bankrupted the USSR

The most popular American narrative: Reagan's massive military buildup — the 600-ship navy, the Strategic Defense Initiative, the Afghanistan proxy war — forced the Soviets to match spending they couldn't afford.

The evidence for this is compelling. The CIA estimated during the Cold War that the USSR was spending 20% of GNP on defense. After the Cold War, when real data became available, that figure turned out to be at least 40-50%, with some estimates reaching 70% if you include military-adjacent infrastructure. Compare this to the US at under 8%, Germany at under 6%, Japan at under 2%.

"If you add up the GNPs of the United States, NATO allies, and Japan, that would be seven times larger than the Soviet GNP, and you gotta be aware of asymmetric strategy."

— Sarah Paine

Paine's verdict: true as far as it goes, but Reagan was building on a foundation laid by Nixon, Ford, and Carter. The arms pressure was cumulative, not sudden.

Argument 2 Nixon played the China card — and it forced fatal overextension

This is Paine's favorite underappreciated argument. In 1969, China and the Soviet Union fought a border war. China, which had gotten its nuclear bomb in 1964, no longer needed to defer to Moscow — and started pushing hard on disputed border territory. The Soviets, alarmed, quietly asked the US whether they'd object to a preemptive nuclear strike on China.

The US said no. China figured out that the power most willing to nuke them was their new primary adversary. This flipped the entire geopolitical triangle: suddenly, the US could cozy up to China, and the Soviets had to militarize not just their European flank but an enormous border with China as well.

"Imagine if this country had to have such borders with Canada and Mexico. It would be bankrupting. And we are far richer than the Soviet Union ever was."

— Sarah Paine

The Soviet military was stretched across two massive frontiers simultaneously — a resource drain that no amount of central planning could fully absorb.

Argument 3 The Helsinki Accords accidentally planted a human rights movement

In the mid-1970s, the Soviets pushed hard for a pan-European conference to officially ratify their World War II border gains. Western Europeans, tired of Cold War tension, agreed — and insisted on including human rights provisions. The US thought this was naive. The Soviets thought the provisions were meaningless formalities they could ignore.

They were both wrong. Within a few years, dissidents across Eastern Europe were using the Helsinki Accords as a legal framework to hold communist governments accountable, publicizing the gap between what the regimes promised and what they delivered. This human rights movement became structurally corrosive to communist legitimacy over the following decade.

Robert Gates, later CIA director and Secretary of Defense, called it the outcome that "yielded benefits beyond our wildest imagination." The Soviets had lobbied for the very conference that would undermine their empire's ideological foundation.

Argument 4 Poland, Solidarity, and the Roman Catholic Church broke the Eastern Bloc from within

The Polish trade union Solidarity — backed by the Roman Catholic Church and the papacy of John Paul II — represented something the Soviets had no model for: a mass civil society movement that was explicitly non-violent and deeply rooted in national identity and religious faith.

Paine argues that the Polish example was the crack that let the light in across the Eastern Bloc. Once it became clear that a large-scale, sustained popular movement could exist and survive in a Warsaw Pact country, the psychological assumption of Soviet inevitability began to erode everywhere from East Germany to Czechoslovakia to Hungary.

This internal pressure compounded the external economic and military stress — and meant that by the late 1980s, Gorbachev was trying to reform a system that was already losing the faith of its own populations.

Argument 5 The economy of lies: central planning created a system that couldn’t tell the truth to itself

Perhaps Paine's most structurally interesting argument: the Soviet command economy was fundamentally incompatible with accurate information. Factory managers reported what the plan required them to report, not what was actually happening. Regional officials submitted numbers that protected their careers. The Politburo made decisions based on data that had been systematically distorted at every level below them.

The result was an economy that couldn't allocate resources rationally because it had no honest price signals and no honest production data. By the time Gorbachev came to power, the Soviet Union had missed the plastics revolution, the semiconductor revolution, and was falling further behind the West each year — while its own statistics continued to show steady growth.

"The belief that we are a great country is deeply ingrained in me. But great in what? Territory? Population? Quantity of arms? People's troubles? The individual's lack of rights?"

— Eduard Shevardnadze, Gorbachev's Foreign Minister, as quoted by Paine

This is the argument Paine finds most historically durable: the Soviet system didn't just lose a geopolitical competition. It collapsed under the weight of its own structural inability to generate and act on accurate information.

Why this matters now

Paine explicitly frames this lecture as relevant to the present. She opens by noting that "we seem to be in a second Cold War," and her conclusion is that the first Cold War's outcome wasn't determined by any single factor but by a decades-long accumulation of strategic decisions, economic contradictions, and ideological erosion that eventually became impossible to paper over.

The statesmanship that ended the Cold War — George H.W. Bush navigating German reunification, Helmut Kohl's patient coalition-building, even Gorbachev's relative restraint — required leaders who understood the system they were operating in and could make decisions at the system level rather than the headline level. Whether today's leaders are capable of the same is, she implies, an open question.
